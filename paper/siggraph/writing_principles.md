# Writing Principles — Internalized from User Critique

These principles are derived from corrections received during the rewrite of this paper. They govern all subsequent revisions and serve as the rubric for reviewer subagents.

## Subject and framing

1. **Lead with the subject, not the framing.** Open paragraphs and sections with the concrete subject (a yōkai, an apparatus, an observation). Defer comparative or theoretical framing until the subject is established.

2. **Establish interest from inside the subject.** Make the reader find the subject itself compelling before introducing the research question. Do not argue interest by external comparison.

## Factual discipline

3. **Every factual claim must be backed by literature or by primary data.** Do not assert that a tradition "does not do X" or "lacks Y" without comparative literature citation.

4. **Verify claims against actual evidence.** If asserting "the cultural form did not ask who was responsible," check first: many yōkai traditions explicitly attribute (tanuki, kitsune, etc.). Claims must survive a folklore-trained reader.

5. **Avoid binary contrasts that overgeneralize either side.** "Japan vs Europe" framings collapse internal diversity in both. Where comparison is needed, name the specific tradition and the specific claim.

6. **Do not claim absences that are factually false.** If a claim of absence would be falsified by one counterexample, it is too strong.

## Rhetorical discipline

7. **Avoid excessive contrasts.** No "X not Y" rhetorical pivots when a positive statement of Y is available.

8. **Avoid vague words.** "Apparatus" is acceptable (concrete). "Scaffold," "operation," "intermediate representation device" are vague unless precisely defined.

9. **Avoid abbreviations.** Use only universally accepted ones (AI in published context).

10. **Reduce em-dashes.** Prefer commas, colons, semicolons. Em-dashes signal a tone we do not want.

11. **Avoid English academic-AI phrasing.** No "We propose," "In this paper we present," "Our contribution is."

## Argument discipline

12. **Topic sentence governs paragraph content.** The paragraph delivers exactly what the topic sentence promises, nothing more.

13. **Each line has clear intent.** Removing any single sentence should weaken the argument. If removal does not weaken, the sentence is inertial.

14. **No inertial repetition.** Do not restate prior content in different words for rhythm.

15. **No confusing layered logic.** Do not stack three or more theoretical concepts when one suffices. Decoration is not argument.

## Statistics

16. **Use statistics to support specific claims.** Numbers must do work. The reader should not have to guess what a percentage demonstrates.

17. **Distinguish proof-of-existence from population estimate.** Small n is sufficient for the former, not the latter. State which is being claimed.

## Reader consideration

18. **Consider the reviewer's labor.** Every sentence should reduce the reviewer's work to understand and assess. Compression is courtesy when it preserves meaning.

19. **No name-dropping.** Cite a work only when the work does specific work for the argument. Reduce, do not pad.

## Self-check

Before submitting any section for review, verify:
- Each topic sentence is followed by content that matches it.
- Each claim has either a citation or a primary-data reference.
- No comparative claim is made without literature.
- No vague abstract terms unless previously defined.
- No em-dashes except where absolutely necessary.
- No formulaic English academic phrasings.
- Removing any sentence weakens the argument.
